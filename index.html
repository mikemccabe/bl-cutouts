A Million Cutout Engravings

Every illustration the British Library cut out of its scanned books, searchable by describing it — and liftable off the page as clip art.

Querying…

Live, from your browser. Click any result for the full-resolution cutout; the toggle shows the loose crop it was lifted out of.

What you can do with it

  • Find pictures by describing them. Nothing here is tagged or catalogued by subject, yet “a platypus” works, and so does “ducks swimming on a pond” or “a hot air balloon”. Matching is on the content of the image itself.
  • Lift an engraving straight off the page. Each result comes back masked, with the body text, caption and page edge that surrounded it removed — usable as public-domain clip art without any tracing or cleanup.
  • Use anything you find, for anything. The whole collection is public domain, so there is no licence to clear and no generated-image ambiguity to explain.
  • Query it without downloading it. A public service answers a search in about half a second and composites cutouts on demand; the images themselves run to 621 GB and can stay where they are.
  • Or take the data and do your own. The masks, the vectors behind the search, and the full OCR text of the same books — 14 million pages of it — are all published files you can read in place with SQL. See the recipes below.

Where it comes from

  • The British Library, with Microsoft, digitised 49,455 books published c. 1510–1900 and released 1,080,814 pictures cut from their pages into the public domain.
  • The cutting was done by software, so the crops are loose: body text above the picture, a printed caption below, the edge of the facing page down one side.
  • As of August 2026, 1,019,266 of them carry a predicted mask — the outline of the picture within its crop. Total compute to outline the collection: $3.24.
  • Search works by fingerprinting every image as a vector and embedding your phrase into the same space, so the vocabulary is open rather than a fixed tag list.
  • Caveats: the masks are model predictions, not hand-checked; publication dates are unreliable at the edges; and the 1890s alone account for a third of the corpus, so this records what one institution digitised rather than what was printed.

Access recipes

What is published

  • biglam/british-library-book-images, CC0. Image configs embellishments (416,935), plates (385,231), medium (217,100), covers (61,548). Fields image, date (a string), fname, image_type. Full images total about 621 GB.
  • siglip2_embeddings — one 1152-dimensional vector per image from google/siglip2-so400m-patch16-256, row-aligned with the image configs. It is a dual encoder, so text embeds into the same space and ranking is cosine similarity. About 5 GB.
  • crop_masks — 3,022,916 instances over 1,019,266 images, one row per image, joined on fname: objects (bbox, score, area, rectangularity) and masks_rle, COCO RLE in the full source frame. 3.96 GB. covers is excluded, the model being unable to abstain.
  • Scores were kept down to 0.10 so consumers choose their own operating point; 59.7% of instances fall below 0.3. Filter at score >= 0.3 for display. 76% of images resolve to exactly one instance.

Vector search over the web, without downloading

Cross-origin requests are reflected for any origin, so a static page can call the service directly — which is what the search above does.

B=https://davanstrien-bl-images-search.hf.space
EID=$(curl -s -X POST "$B/gradio_api/call/search" \
  -H 'content-type: application/json' \
  -d '{"data":["a platypus","all",0,0,8]}' | jq -r .event_id)
curl -s -N "$B/gradio_api/call/search/$EID"
  • /search — query, collection, year_from, year_to, k
  • /search_reference — adds seed_row and text_weight, blending a phrase with an example image
  • /search_feedback — adds positive_rows and negative_rows
  • Schemas at /gradio_api/info and /openapi.json

Single images and cutouts by URL

https://huggingface.co/buckets/biglam/bl-images/resolve/{thumbs|full}/{split}/{fname}
{space}/cutout?fname={fname}&size={thumb|full}    # masked PNG, composited on demand

Pixels outside the mask come back white rather than transparent. That region is connected to the frame, so a flood fill inward from the border converts exactly it to alpha.

SQL against the parquet, in place

DuckDB reads the published files directly with column pruning, so no image bytes move unless the image column is selected.

SELECT i.fname, i.date, m.objects, m.masks_rle
FROM 'hf://datasets/biglam/british-library-book-images/plates/*.parquet' i
JOIN 'hf://datasets/biglam/british-library-book-images/crop-masks/plates-*.parquet' m
USING (fname)

The leading digits of fname are the British Library system number, which is also the record_id of the OCR corpus described next.

The page text of the same books

The same digitisation programme also released its OCR, and it is genuinely full text rather than metadata or snippets: biglam/blbooks-parquet, 14,011,953 rows, one per page, about 30 GB, CC0. Each row carries the page's text and pg number, an empty_pg flag, per-page OCR word confidence as mean_wc_ocr and std_wc_ocr, and catalogue metadata (title, name, place, Publisher, date, Language_1..4). English, German, Spanish, French, Italian and Dutch.

-- the text of the book an illustration was cut from
SELECT pg, text
FROM 'hf://datasets/biglam/blbooks-parquet/data/train-*.parquet'
WHERE record_id = '002253078'      -- leading digits of the image's fname
  AND NOT empty_pg
  AND mean_wc_ocr > 0.8           -- drop the pages OCR made a mess of
ORDER BY pg

The join is at book level, not page level. An image's fname does encode a page position, but it is not guaranteed to line up with the pg column here, so treat page-level alignment as something to verify rather than assume. In a 20,000-row sample, 96% of system numbers found a match: the remaining 4% are books present in the image release but absent from the OCR release.

Two things to expect. The text is raw OCR of nineteenth-century print with no correction pass — confidence below 0.5 is common and often unusable, which is what the word-confidence columns are for. And the metadata is a 2021 catalogue export: several fields are unpopulated much of the time, and the language fields were partly determined computationally.

One caution: the cutout endpoint is not concurrency-safe

Sixteen cutout requests issued in parallel returned a mixture of 404 no mask for this image and 502 thumb fetch failed; the same thirty-six URLs fetched one at a time returned thirty-six successes. The 404 comes from a shared database handle used across threads, so a mask that exists is reported missing. Serialise the requests, or run two at a time with exponential backoff, and retry rather than trusting the status.

The illustrations are public domain, with no known copyright restrictions; attribution to the British Library is expected practice rather than a legal condition. Nothing in the collection has been reviewed for offensive content, and colonial-era publishing is heavily represented. Queries and cutouts are fetched by your browser from the search service, which sleeps when idle, so the first search after a quiet period may take a minute.
